Why it matters

One missed test affects three groups of people

The patient

A treatment that might work much better

MSI-high tumours are covered in DNA errors, so the immune system can recognise them. Immunotherapy helps it do that. Without the test, it is never considered.

The family

A chance to prevent cancer, not just treat it

Some MSI-high cancers come from Lynch syndrome. Relatives who learn they carry it can start colonoscopy early and have polyps removed before they turn into cancer.

Research

Trials that can find their patients

Trials of new drugs for MSI-high cancer need these patients. Untested patients are invisible to them.

Same tumour, two outcomes

The difference is whether anyone looks again

Who should be tested

One result can protect a whole family

Lynch syndrome is passed down in families. When one person is found to carry it, close relatives can take a simple blood or saliva test for the same gene change. This is called cascade testing.

Relatives who test negative go back to average risk. Relatives who test positive can start colonoscopy early, when polyps can be removed before they become cancer.

On average a person has about four first-degree relatives (parents, siblings, children), and about half are likely to share the family's gene change (Washington State DOH).

How family testing works

From one result to a protected family

  1. Get the patient's genetic report

    It names the gene and the exact change, and confirms it causes disease.

  2. See a genetic counsellor

    They review family history and explain what each result would mean.

  3. Relatives give a blood or saliva sample

    The lab looks only for the family's known change, which is quicker and cheaper.

  4. Act on the result

    Negative: normal screening. Positive: colonoscopy every 1–2 years from age 25, plus screening for other Lynch cancers.

  5. Pass it on

    Each relative who tests positive tells their own children and siblings.

Questions to ask your care team

Bring these to your next appointment

  • Was my tumour tested for MSI or mismatch repair (MMR)?
  • If not, can it be tested on the tissue you already have?
  • If it is MSI-high or MMR-deficient, could immunotherapy be an option for me?
  • Should I have genetic counselling or testing for Lynch syndrome?
  • Which of my relatives should be tested, and how do I tell them?

General information, not medical advice. Talk to your doctor or a genetic counsellor about your own situation. Sources: Moffitt Cancer Center, Washington State DOH, CION Cancer Clinics, CURE.

For clinicians & labs

A background safety net for MMR/MSI testing

Histofinder runs on slides your lab already scans and produces a ranked worklist of untested patients. It never replaces IHC or PCR. It makes sure the right patients reach them.

What happens after a flag

An MMR-deficient tumour without BRAF V600E or MLH1 promoter methylation suggests a hereditary cause and warrants genetic counselling (CURE). Follow your institution's protocol.

Why rule-in is safer

It can only add tests, never remove them

Histofinder runs only on patients who were not going to be tested. When it flags someone, a standard test decides. When it misses someone, that patient is exactly where they would have been without it.

Questions clinicians ask

FAQ

Does Histofinder replace MMR immunohistochemistry or MSI PCR?

No. It decides who should be tested among patients who were not. Every flag is confirmed with a standard test before any treatment or genetic decision.

Which specimens does it work on?

The research models are trained on diagnostic (FFPE) H&E slides of colorectal and stomach cancers from TCGA. Biopsies and other scanners need separate validation, which is part of the pilot plan.

How accurate is it?

See the performance section of the Demo page. Those are held-out test-set numbers from one public dataset (TCGA colorectal and stomach); validation on your own slides is the first step of any pilot.

Does patient data leave the hospital?

For a pilot, Histofinder can run inside your environment on de-identified slides under a data use agreement, set up with your IT and privacy teams.

Is it FDA cleared?

No. It is a research prototype for research use only.

About

Why Histofinder exists

The information that decides a cancer patient's treatment, and sometimes their family's future, is often already sitting on a glass slide. Histofinder exists so that no one misses it because a test was never ordered.
